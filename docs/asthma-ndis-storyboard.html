1. Analysis summary

Item Detail
Learners Disability support workers in NDIS in-home support. They often work alone in a participant's home. Their team leader is usually on the phone, not in the room.
Performance gap Workers may write off early signs as a cold or one-off, give extra doses outside the plan, take over tasks and remove independence, leave the reliever behind, use out-of-date or poorly cleaned devices, wait to act on the action plan, or phone the team leader before calling 000.
Context The participant's own asthma action plan always comes first. Workers act within their provider's medication policy, their training and the participant's support plan. Liam's choice and control run through every lesson: Jess supports his decisions and explains risks in ways he understands.
Instructional strategy Five branching scenarios. Each follows the same pattern: Situation, Decision point (three options), Consequences, Debrief, On the floor.
Cast Jess Kowalski (the learner), Daniel Osei (team leader), Liam Carter (participant), Karen Carter (Liam's mum).
Delivery Not stated in the source. See “Design decisions to confirm” below.

2. Design blueprint

2.1 Course flow

Course intro (how the scenarios work, cast, scope note) > Lesson > Situation > Decision point > Consequence A, B or C > Debrief > On the floor > Next lesson.

2.2 Objectives and the decision that evidences each

Lesson Title and situation Objective (the learner can...) Stronger choice and why
1 What asthma is: “Just a cold” Recognise the everyday signs that a participant's asthma is not well controlled, even when they don't say so, and report them. Option B. Option B describes facts, not guesses, so the team leader, family and GP can act early.
2 Triggers: The garden centre trip Identify a participant's asthma triggers, and reduce exposure while respecting their choice and control. Option B. Option B shares the risk in a way Liam understands, gives him real options, and plans for safety whichever he picks.
3 Medications and devices: The new preventer Tell a preventer from a reliever, spot device problems, and support good technique within your role. Option B. Option B fixes the problems, follows the medication chart and builds Liam's own skills.
4 Action plans: Getting worse Read a participant's written asthma action plan, match their symptoms to the right section and act on it. Option B. Option B matches the symptoms to the right section, follows it, and keeps Liam, his family and the team informed.
5 Asthma attack first aid: Alone in the garden Recognise a severe asthma attack, call 000 and give asthma first aid while working alone, then report the incident. Option B. Option B follows the steps of asthma first aid and gets help on the way early.

2.4 Design principles taken from the source

Item Detail
Choice and control Jess supports Liam's decisions and explains risks in ways he understands. The stronger option in lessons 2 and 3 keeps Liam in charge. Do not write consequences that treat participant choice as the mistake.
Working alone Each decision tests what Jess does on her own and when she escalates. Lesson 5 makes the point most strongly: 000 and first aid come before calling the team leader.
Scope Workers follow the participant's action plan, the medication chart and their provider's medication policy, and only do medication tasks they are trained and authorised to do.
Accessible communication Liam speaks in short sentences and likes things explained with pictures. Plans and guides should be available in picture or easy read form. Keep Liam's lines short, as written.
Feedback Consequence first (what happens), then the debrief explains why. Every option's debrief is written to explain, not to blame.
Tone Collegial and practical. Australian spelling. Daniel's feedback is direct but supportive.

2.6 Design decisions to confirm

The source document sets out the content and the scenario pattern, but not the build. These need an answer before development.

Decision Options and recommendation
Delivery platform Browser-based branching scenario, LMS module (SCORM or xAPI), or facilitated sessions. Not stated in the source.
Voice Recorded actors (recommended, since Liam's speech and Jess's tone matter) or synthesised voices. Casting for Liam should involve people with lived experience.
Visual style Illustrated characters, photographed scenes or video. Set a style guide: palette, type, scene backgrounds.
Question wording The source gives three options at each decision point but no question stem. Suggested stem: “What should Jess do?” (lesson 5: “What should Jess do first?”).
Replay Allow learners to try the other options after the debrief. Recommended.
Accessible version for the learners Captions, transcripts, keyboard use, screen reader order, and plain-language text.
Easy read participant materials The course tells workers to ask for accessible plans and picture guides. Consider supplying a sample picture card for Liam's spacer routine.

L1 Debrief (L1-DB)

Item Detail
Stronger choice Option B
Summary Asthma is a long-term condition where the airways become swollen and narrow. When it isn't well controlled, the signs are often small and spread across days.
Why: option A Option A treats a pattern as a one-off. Small signs that aren't reported can build into an emergency.
Why: option B Option B describes facts, not guesses, so the team leader, family and GP can act early.
Why: option C Option C is well-meant, but it's outside Liam's plan and outside Jess's role. It masks the warning signs.
Note Common signs of poorly controlled asthma include coughing or waking at night, needing the reliever more often than usual, and avoiding activities that used to be easy. Many participants won't say “I'm short of breath”. They may stop, sit down, seem irritable or tired, or avoid stairs.
On the floor

• Learn what's normal for each participant, so you notice when it changes.

• Write what you see and hear, with times: “sat down after 5 minutes of weeding, breathing fast”, not “has a cold”.

• Check the reliever's dose counter when you check other medications.

• Report a pattern to your team leader on the same shift.

• Ask the participant in simple words: “Is breathing hard?” and watch for nods, gestures or behaviour.

L2 Debrief (L2-DB)

Item Detail
Stronger choice Option B
Summary Reducing exposure to triggers helps prevent flare-ups, but the participant still makes the decisions about their own life.
Why: option A Option A keeps Liam safe today, but takes away his choice without explaining why.
Why: option B Option B shares the risk in a way Liam understands, gives him real options, and plans for safety whichever he picks.
Why: option C Option C respects his choice, but ignores a known high-risk trigger and leaves him without his reliever.
Note Common asthma triggers include colds and flu, pollen, smoke (cigarette, bushfire and wood fire), cold air, dust, mould, strong smells such as sprays and perfume, exercise and stress. Each participant's triggers are different, so check their support plan and ask them and their family. In Victoria, epidemic thunderstorm asthma can happen in grass pollen season, usually October to December.
On the floor

• Know each participant's triggers, as listed in their support plan.

• In spring, check the thunderstorm asthma and pollen forecast at the start of your shift.

• Explain risks with plain words, pictures or the participant's preferred communication style.

• Offer real choices, and record what the participant decided and why.

• Make the reliever and spacer go everywhere the participant goes.

Medicines and devices table

Preventer. What it does: Reduces swelling in the airways over time. Taken every day, even when well. What to check: Taken as prescribed, mouth rinsed after if the chart says so

Reliever (usually blue or grey). What it does: Opens the airways within minutes. Used for symptoms and first aid. What to check: In date, dose counter not near zero, always with the participant

Spacer. What it does: Helps more medicine reach the lungs What to check: Clean, drip-dried, not cracked, goes everywhere with the reliever
On the floor

• Follow the participant's medication chart and your provider's medication policy.

• Only support medication tasks you've been trained and authorised to do.

• Check expiry dates and dose counters on all puffers each week.

• Wash spacers in warm soapy water and let them drip-dry.

• Ask for a pharmacist or GP technique check if you notice a problem.

Asthma first aid with a blue or grey reliever puffer

1. Sit the person upright, stay with them and be calm and reassuring.

2. Give 4 separate puffs of the reliever through a spacer: 1 puff at a time, with 4 breaths after each puff.

3. Wait 4 minutes. If they still can't breathe normally, give 4 more puffs.

4. If they still can't breathe normally, call 000. Keep giving 4 puffs every 4 minutes until the ambulance arrives.
Note Call 000 straight away if the person is not breathing, gets suddenly worse, has severe signs (such as only speaking a few words, or blue or pale lips), has no reliever, or you're not sure it's asthma. If the participant's action plan or reliever uses different first aid steps, follow those.
On the floor

• Know where the reliever, spacer and action plan are at the start of every shift.

• In an emergency, call 000 before calling your team leader.

• Use your phone's speaker, so you can call and give first aid at the same time.

• After the emergency, tell your team leader and complete an incident report through your provider's system. Your team leader will decide whether it needs to be reported to the NDIS Quality and Safeguards Commission.

• Look after yourself afterwards. Talk to your team leader or your employee support line.
