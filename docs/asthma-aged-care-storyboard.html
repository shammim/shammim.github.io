1. Analysis summary

Item Detail
Target learners Registered nurses and care staff in aged care, time-poor, with mixed experience of asthma.
Performance gap Staff normalise night-time symptoms, miss triggers, mishandle inhaler devices, follow out-of-date action plans and hesitate in a severe attack.
Delivery Browser-based, self-paced. Browser speech provides character voices, with Pause, Next line and Restart controls. Progress is saved in the browser.
Constraints Accessibility (keyboard, screen reader, reduced motion, light and dark themes), no scoring, clinical content aligned with national guidance and local policy.
Instructional strategy Branching scenario. Each lesson shows a situation, asks for one decision (A, B or C), plays the consequence, then gives a debrief and 'On the floor' actions. Learners can replay other options.

2. Design blueprint

2.1 Course flow

Lesson tab > Intro (outcome and cast) > Situation > Decision > Branch A, B or C (consequence) > Debrief > Try another option, or Next lesson.

L1 Debrief (L1-DB)

Element Content
Verdict 'You chose Option X, the stronger choice.' or 'You chose Option X. Option B is the stronger choice.' (green or amber text)
Summary Asthma is a long-term condition where the airways become inflamed and narrow. Night-time or early-morning symptoms are a sign it isn't well controlled, so they need to be reported, not normalised.
Rationale: Option A Option A is common because breathlessness in older people is often blamed on age, heart failure or COPD.
Rationale: Option B Option B recognises the signs, checks the resident's actual diagnosis and plan, and escalates for review.
Rationale: Option C Option C is well-meant, but regular reliever use without a review masks worsening asthma.
On the floor

- Know which residents in your area have asthma. Check the diagnosis, not just the symptoms.

- Watch for the four main signs: wheeze, cough, chest tightness and shortness of breath.

- Treat symptoms at night or early morning as a warning sign, and document the pattern.

- Report repeated symptoms or increased reliever use, and request a GP review.

L2 Debrief (L2-DB)

Element Content
Verdict 'You chose Option X, the stronger choice.' or 'You chose Option X. Option B is the stronger choice.' (green or amber text)
Summary Reducing exposure to triggers is one of the simplest ways to prevent flare-ups. Recording them makes the whole team part of the solution.
Rationale: Option A Option A treats symptoms but ignores the cause, and makes things worse on a smoke day.
Rationale: Option B Option B manages symptoms now, removes the triggers, and records them so every shift knows.
Rationale: Option C Option C helps in one room, but without documentation the knowledge leaves with the shift.
Note Common triggers in aged care include respiratory infections, smoke from bushfires or wood heaters, cold air, dust and mould, strong scents and cleaning products, and some medicines. In Melbourne, spring also brings the risk of thunderstorm asthma on high-pollen days.
On the floor

- Ask “what's changed?” when a resident's breathing changes suddenly.

- Check air quality and pollen alerts, and keep windows closed when smoke or pollen is high.

- Use unscented products around residents with asthma, and avoid aerosol sprays.

- Record each resident's known triggers in their care plan and mention them at handover.

- Encourage flu and other recommended vaccinations, since infections are a major trigger.

First aid steps (adults)

1. Sit the person upright. Stay calm and reassure them.

2. Give 4 separate puffs through a spacer: 1 puff, then 4 breaths, until 4 puffs are given.

3. Wait 4 minutes, staying with them.

4. Still not breathing normally? Give 4 more puffs. If breathing is still not normal, call 000 and keep giving 4 puffs every 4 minutes until the ambulance arrives.
Note Call 000 straight away if the breathing problem is severe, the person is drowsy or looks blue around the lips, is getting worse or not improving, or you're not sure what to do. If someone with asthma and a severe allergy has sudden breathing trouble, give adrenaline first, then the reliever.
