How to use these

  • Print on A4. Laminate the first aid page (5) and display it where relievers and spacers are kept.

  • Use aids 1 to 4 at handover, medication rounds and care plan reviews. They are prompts, not a replacement for the resident's plan.

  • Fill in the tick-boxes and tables in aids 2, 3, 4 and 5 for your own residents, then file the page with the care plan.

  • Before you roll these out, have your clinical lead check them against current guidance and your organisation's policies.

JOB AID 1 · LESSON 1

Spot the signs

Asthma in older residents: recognise it, record the pattern, report it

Four signs to watch for

Wheeze Cough Chest tightness Shortness of breath
Whistling or noisy breathing Dry or persistent, often worse at night “Like a belt around my chest” Out of breath at rest or with little effort

Night-time or early-morning symptoms mean asthma isn't well controlled.

Don't write them off as age, the heart or COPD. Check the resident's actual diagnosis in their chart.

What's happening in the airways

The airways become inflamed and swollen, the muscles around them tighten and they make extra mucus. There is less room for air.

Do and don't

Do

  • Check the diagnosis, not just the symptoms.

  • Document the pattern: how many nights, what time, what you saw.

  • Check the asthma action plan.

  • Report repeated symptoms or more reliever use, and ask for a GP review.

Don't

  • Record it as “probably just his age”.

  • Assume it's COPD or the heart without checking the chart.

  • Give the reliever every night as a routine. It hides the pattern and needs a review.

Handover prompt

Resident: ______________ Symptoms: ☐ wheeze ☐ cough ☐ chest tightness ☐ short of breath

Nights in a row: ____ Time(s): ____________ Reliever used? ☐ No ☐ Yes, how often: ______

Action plan checked ☐ GP review requested ☐ Handed over to: ______________

JOB AID 2 · LESSON 2

Triggers: what's changed?

Remove the trigger. Write it down.

When breathing changes suddenly, ask: “What's changed?”

Triggers stack up. Look at the room, the air, the products, the weather and whether the resident is unwell.

Common triggers in aged care

Trigger Examples and what to do
Strong scents and sprays Lavender spray, cleaning products, aerosols. Use unscented products. Avoid aerosol sprays.
Smoke Bushfire or planned-burn haze, wood heaters. Check air quality notices. Keep windows closed on smoke-alert days.
Infections Colds, flu and other respiratory infections. Encourage flu and other recommended vaccinations.
Cold air, dust and mould Check rooms and ventilation. Keep dust down.
Pollen and thunderstorms On high-pollen days, thunderstorm asthma can be a risk, especially in spring. Check pollen alerts and keep windows closed.
Some medicines, reflux, stress Different for every resident. Check the care plan and talk to the GP or pharmacist.

Steps

  1. Manage the symptoms now, following the resident's action plan.

  2. Remove or reduce the trigger (close the window, stop the spray).

  3. Ask other teams to help, for example housekeeping.

  4. Record the trigger in the care plan and mention it at handover.

JOB AID 3 · LESSON 3

Medicines and devices

A dose given isn't always a dose received

Asthma medicines at a glance

Type What it does
Reliever (blue/grey puffer, e.g. salbutamol) Quickly relaxes the airway muscles to ease symptoms. Needing it more often is a warning sign.
Preventer (inhaled corticosteroid) Reduces inflammation to prevent attacks. Take every day, even when well, then rinse, gargle and spit.
Combination inhaler Controls inflammation and opens the airways. Some are used as both preventer and reliever, so follow the action plan.

Technique check (watch every resident use their device)

  • Dry powder inhalers need a strong, fast breath in. If the resident can't manage it, most of the medicine stays in the mouth and throat.

  • A puffer with a spacer gets more medicine into the lungs for most older people. Use a spacer wherever the resident has one.

  • If technique is poor, document it and ask the GP and pharmacist about a device that suits the resident.

  • Check dose counters and expiry dates. Report residents who can't manage their device.

After every preventer dose

Rinse, gargle and spit.

Preventers contain a steroid. Without a rinse, residents can get oral thrush. Sore mouth or white patches on the tongue: tell the nurse in charge and ask the GP to look.

Cleaning a spacer

  • Wash in warm soapy water.

  • Don't rinse.

  • Let it air dry.

  • Don't dry with a towel: static makes the medicine stick to the sides.

Never

  • Skip a prescribed preventer when the resident struggles. Speak to the prescriber.

  • Rely on the reliever alone. It opens the airways for a few hours but doesn't treat the cause.

JOB AID 4 · LESSON 4

Action plan check

Current plan. Same page. Every shift.

What a written asthma action plan covers

It is prepared by the resident's doctor and usually covers three things:

Stage What it tells you
Daily The resident's regular medicines.
Getting worse Signs that asthma is getting worse and what to do. Needing the reliever more than usual means the resident is moving into this stage.
Emergency What to do in an emergency.

Five-point check for each resident with asthma

  1. Is there a written action plan, and do I know where it is kept?

  2. Is it current? Check the date.

  3. Does it match the current medication chart (medicines and doses)?

  4. How often has the resident needed the reliever this week? Which stage are they in?

  5. Has anything changed since the plan was written (flare-up, hospital visit, new medicines)?

If the plan and chart don't match

Do

  • Manage today's symptoms using the current medication chart.

  • Contact the GP and ask for a review today.

  • Request an updated written action plan.

  • Put the new plan in the file and medication folder, and flag it at handover.

Don't

  • Follow an out-of-date plan just because it is signed.

  • Change a dose yourself. Dose changes must come from the prescriber.

Ask for a plan review

At least yearly, after a flare-up or hospital visit, and whenever medicines change.

JOB AID 5 · LESSON 5

Asthma first aid

Call early. Sit upright. One puff, four breaths.

Severe attack signs: call 000 straight away

• Can only say a word or two, or can't speak comfortably

• Drowsy, or blue around the lips

• Getting worse or not improving

• You're not sure what to do

You can always cancel the ambulance. You can't get those minutes back.

First aid with a blue/grey reliever and spacer (adults)

  1. Sit the person upright. Stay with them, stay calm and reassure them. Don't walk them anywhere or lay them down.

  2. Give 4 separate puffs through a spacer: 1 puff, then 4 breaths, until 4 puffs are given.

  3. Wait 4 minutes, staying with them.

  4. Still not breathing normally? Give 4 more puffs. If breathing is still not normal, call 000 and keep giving 4 puffs every 4 minutes until the ambulance arrives.

Special cases

  • Asthma and a severe allergy with sudden breathing trouble: give adrenaline first, then the reliever.

  • If the resident's action plan uses a combination inhaler for first aid, follow their plan.

Avoid

  • Several puffs fired one straight after another. Most hits the back of the throat.

  • Moving the resident or laying them flat. Both make breathing harder.

Dose and time log (hand to the paramedics with the action plan and medication chart)

Resident Time first puff 000 called at Puffs given (time of each round)

Afterwards

  • Document what happened and the time of every dose.

  • Inform the GP and family.

  • Ask for an action plan review.
